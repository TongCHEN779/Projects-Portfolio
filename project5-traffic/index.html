Retime a city and watch the queues

Nine signalised intersections, cars and bikes following an intelligent-driver model, pedestrians who cross on their phase, and traffic that turns — giving way to cyclists and people in the crossing, as it must. Everything appears at random. The interesting part is the panel: change the green time, the yellow, the offset between neighbouring lights, or how many drivers turn, and watch average delay collapse or explode. A green wave is just the right offset — set it and the queues melt.

Red/green dots are the stop-line signals. Blue = cars, teal = bikes, amber = pedestrians. Vehicles turn red when stopped — including turners giving way to a cyclist on their inside, or to someone in the crossing.

What's under the hood. Each car and bike follows the Intelligent Driver Model: a = a₀[1 − (v/v₀)⁴ − (s*/s)²], where the desired gap s* = s₀ + vT + v·Δv/(2√(a₀b)) grows with speed and closing rate. A red light is simply a stationary obstacle at the stop line, so queues, stop-and-go waves and discharge at green all fall out of the same rule rather than being scripted. Bikes use the same model with a lower desired speed and ride their own lane, so they don't block cars.

Why the offset matters. Each intersection runs the same cycle C = 2(g + y + r) but starts it offset·i seconds later along the corridor. If the offset matches the travel time between intersections — roughly distance / speed — a platoon released at one light arrives exactly as the next turns green: a green wave, and average delay drops sharply. Set it to half a cycle instead and every platoon hits a red. Try Green wave vs Bad timing; only the offset changes.

Turning, and who gives way. At every junction each vehicle rolls a route — straight, left or right — and peels off onto a quadratic Bézier arc from its stop line to the corner to the far lane, rejoining the perpendicular lane once it clears the box. A turn is never free, because it always cuts across somebody:

Bikes ride outside the car lane, nearer the kerb, so a right-turning car crosses its own bike lane — the classic right hook, and it must give way to any cyclist going straight on. A left turn is permissive: the driver waits for a gap in oncoming traffic, cars and bikes alike. And both turns drive over the crosswalk of the road they turn into, whose pedestrians are walking on the very phase that just released the turn — so turning vehicles yield to anyone already in the crossing. A driver who has waited too long will force a vehicle gap so the network can't deadlock, but never forces a pedestrian.

The result is that turning traffic is expensive. There are no turn pockets here, so one yielding vehicle blocks everyone queued behind it. Push Turning up and watch a single left-turner — or one waiting on a steady stream of bikes — throttle an entire approach. That is exactly why real junctions buy left-turn lanes, protected arrows and separate cycle phases.

Honest note. Distances and speeds are illustrative rather than calibrated, each approach is a single shared lane, and demand is a homogeneous Poisson process at each entry. Vehicles mid-turn don't check each other inside the box. The point is the qualitative behaviour — how delay responds to timing — not a validated microsimulation.
