Anytime-Certified Bounds for the Moment–SOS Hierarchy

Interior-point solvers run out of memory, not time, on the moment–SOS hierarchy. A spectral dual reformulation turns it into an eigenvalue problem where every iterate is a rigorous bound and memory is $O(s^2)$ instead of $O(s^4)$ — enough to certify order-2 MAX-CUT optimality on graphs of up to 60 nodes, with proofs that survive floating-point rounding and a primal side that brackets the relaxation value. We also report where it loses to the alternatives.

Introduction

Polynomial optimization — maximizing a polynomial over a basic semialgebraic set — is NP-hard, yet admits the convergent moment–SOS hierarchy of Lasserre [1] (see also [2][3][4]). Relaxation order $r$ yields an SDP whose moment matrix has size $\binom{n+r}{r}$ and which involves $\binom{n+2r}{2r}$ moment variables. The SDP is dense and highly structured, but general interior-point solvers exploit this structure poorly: their cost is governed by the number of moments $m\sim s^2$, not by the matrix size $s$, and they must store an $m\times m$ matrix. For MAX-CUT on $n=40$ nodes at order $r=2$, the moment matrix is only $821\times 821$ but $m=102{,}091$; the interior-point solver MOSEK was killed by the operating system during the optimizer phase on a 36 GiB machine.

So interior-point solvers run out of memory, not time, long before the hierarchy is exhausted. This article describes a first-order alternative with two properties that interior-point methods do not have:

  • Anytime certificates. Every iterate yields a rigorous upper bound on the optimum. Stop whenever you like and you still hold a proof — and that proof can be upgraded a posteriori to a bound valid in exact arithmetic.
  • $O(s^2)$ memory instead of $O(s^4)$: one eigen-decomposition per block and a sparse back-substitution.

We are equally explicit about what the method does not do: it is slower than interior-point on small problems, less accurate, and slower than uncertified first-order baselines. The question is what the certificate costs and what it buys.

Related work. Sparsity exploitation (correlative [5][6], term [7][8]) shrinks the blocks and is complementary to our approach. First-order and low-rank solvers for general SDPs include ADMM-type methods [9][10], sketching-based conditional gradient [11], and Burer–Monteiro factorizations [12], recently also tailored to moment–SOS relaxations [13][14]. The reduction of SDPs with a trace bound to the minimization of a largest eigenvalue is classical [15][16]; for the moment–SOS hierarchy it was exploited through the constant trace property by Mai, Lasserre, Magron and Wang [17][18], who solve the resulting eigenvalue problem with bundle methods. Dathathri et al. [19] introduced the diagonal-domination certificate for neural-network verification.

Contributions.

  1. A unified Gram-matrix dual with a certificate theorem. Inequalities, equalities, cliques and Boolean variables are handled uniformly, and every parameter value certifies an upper bound. Non-constant traces are handled by box bounds, with automatically computed localizing weights $B_i$.
  2. Exactness without a duality gap: the infimum of the certified bound is the value of the moment relaxation with valid trace cuts; for Boolean problems it equals the Lasserre value.
  3. Automatic assembly. The Gram matrices are the Hessian blocks of a formal Lagrangian; every oracle call costs one eigen-decomposition per block.
  4. Smoothing with complexity guarantees: a sandwich bound, a gradient with Lipschitz constant $O(1/\eta)$, and an $O(1/\varepsilon)$ iteration bound for accelerated gradient.
  5. A certification corollary: for integer-valued objectives the optimum is bounded by $\lfloor U\rfloor$; with a feasible point this proves optimality.
  6. A rigorous a posteriori certificate: the floating-point output is turned into a bound valid in exact arithmetic, by a verified Cholesky bound on the eigenvalues and an exact rational residual of the Gram identity, at a cost below one oracle call; the correction is below $10^{-8}$ in all experiments.
  7. Primal recovery and a two-sided certificate: the softmax density matrices of the smoothed objective define a moment vector whose infeasibility is exactly the gradient of $U_\eta$; for Boolean problems without constraints a single dual iterate brackets the relaxation value from both sides, with no reference solver.
  8. Diagonal domination and the role of the cuts: minimizing the weighted domination over the weights gives the diagonal-cut relaxation $\rho_r^{\rm d}\le\rho_r^{\rm tr}\le\rho_r$; when the box is not encoded in the constraints the Lasserre relaxation is unbounded while the cut relaxations are finite.
  9. A smoothing-free dual augmented-Lagrangian solver for problems with many constraint blocks.
  10. Honest experiments: agreement with MOSEK on 23 problem–order pairs, scaling up to order 3 and $n=60$, a comparison with SCS, CGAL and Burer–Monteiro, rigorous certificates for all MAX-CUT instances up to $n=40$, primal recovery along the run, the role of the cuts, certified gaps of $10^{-8}$ with the augmented Lagrangian, and the variability over instances.
  11. Second-order relaxations of ReLU-network problems and the PyTorch package polyopt.

Setting and Notation

We consider $$f^\star=\max_{x\in K} f(x),\qquad K=\{x\in\R^n:\ g_i(x)\ge0\ (i=1,\dots,p),\ h_j(x)=0\ (j=1,\dots,q)\}.$$ We assume the box $K\subseteq[-1,1]^n$ (no loss of generality after rescaling). In the Boolean case $K\subseteq\{\pm1\}^n$ and all computations are done modulo the ideal $\langle x_i^2-1\rangle$: monomials are squarefree and products are symmetric differences of supports.

Cliques. Let $I_1,\dots,I_N\subseteq[n]$ be index sets (a single clique $I_1=[n]$ is the dense case) such that every $g_i,h_j$ lies in some clique and every monomial of $f$ is a product of two monomials of one clique. For each clique $v_k$ is the vector of monomials in $x_{I_k}$ of degree $\le r$ and $s_k=|v_k|$. For an inequality $g_i$, $z_i$ is the vector of monomials of degree $\le r-\lceil\deg g_i/2\rceil$ and $s_i=|z_i|$. We require $\deg f\le2r$. Let $$B_i:=\sum_{\alpha:\,c_\alpha>0}c_\alpha+\sum_{\alpha:\,c_\alpha<0,\ \alpha\ \text{not even}}|c_\alpha|\ \ \ge \sup_{[-1,1]^n}g_i\qquad\text{for }g_i=\sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha .$$ This is a valid, automatable upper bound on $\sup g_i$ over the box, sharper than the usual $\sum_\alpha|c_\alpha|$; e.g. $B=1$ for $g=1-x^2$.

Moment relaxation. For a vector $y$ indexed by monomials, $L_y$ is the linear functional with $L_y(x^\gamma)=y_\gamma$; $M_k(y)=L_y(v_kv_k^\top)$ and $M_i^{\rm loc}(y)=L_y(g_iz_iz_i^\top)$ are the moment and localizing matrices. The order-$r$ Lasserre relaxation $\rho_r$ maximizes $L_y(f)$ subject to $y_0=1$, $M_k(y)\succeq0$, $M^{\rm loc}_i(y)\succeq0$ and $L_y(h_jx^\mu)=0$. Let $\rho_r^{\rm tr}$ be the same problem with the additional trace cuts $$\tr M_k(y)\le s_k,\qquad \tr M_i^{\rm loc}(y)\le s_iB_i,$$ which hold for every moment vector of a probability measure on $K$ because $|x^\alpha|\le1$ on the box and $0\le g_i\le B_i$ on $K$. Hence $f^\star\le\rho_r^{\rm tr}\le\rho_r$. In the Boolean case $M_k(y)_{aa}=y_0=1$, so $\tr M_k=s_k$ identically (“constant trace”) and the first cut is redundant.

Gram Certificates and the Spectral Dual

Definition (dual point). A dual point $\theta$ consists of a scalar $\sigma$, symmetric matrices $Q_{0,k}\in\Sym^{s_k}$, $Q_i\in\Sym^{s_i}$ — not required to be positive semidefinite — and polynomials $p_j$ such that, modulo the ideal in the Boolean case, $$\sigma-f \;=\; \sum_{k}v_k^\top Q_{0,k}v_k+\sum_{i}\big(z_i^\top Q_iz_i\big)g_i+\sum_jp_jh_j .$$

With $\Phi(Q)=\pos{-\lmin(Q)}$, define the spectral bound $$U(\theta)=\sigma+\sum_ks_k\,\Phi_k(Q_{0,k})+\sum_is_iB_i\,\pos{-\lmin(Q_i)},$$ where $\Phi_k(Q)=-\lmin(Q)$ (without the positive part) if clique $k$ is Boolean.

Theorem 1 (certificate). For every dual point $\theta$ and every $x\in K$, $f(x)\le U(\theta)$. Hence $f^\star\le U(\theta)$.

Proof. Fix $x\in K$ and $\lambda_k=\Phi_k(Q_{0,k})$. Then $Q_{0,k}+\lambda_kI\succeq0$, so $v_k^\top Q_{0,k}v_k\ge-\lambda_k\|v_k\|^2$. On the box $\|v_k(x)\|^2\le s_k$, and in the Boolean case it equals $s_k$ exactly, so $v_k^\top Q_{0,k}v_k\ge-\lambda_ks_k$ holds even if $\lambda_k<0$. For an inequality, put $\mu_i=\pos{-\lmin(Q_i)}$; since $g_i(x)\in[0,B_i]$ and $\|z_i\|^2\le s_i$, $(z_i^\top Q_iz_i)g_i\ge-\mu_is_iB_i$. Equality terms vanish on $K$. Evaluating the identity at $x$ gives $\sigma-f(x)\ge-\sum_ks_k\lambda_k-\sum_is_iB_i\mu_i$. $\square$

The dual point is free: no PSD constraint on any $Q$. The price of non-PSD is repaid by a multiple of the identity, whose size is the (smallest) eigenvalue violation — that is the whole spectral bound. Play with the simplest example: maximize $x$ subject to $1-x^2\ge0$ at order $1$. Here $v=(1,x)$, $z=1$, $B=1$, $s_0=2$, $s_1=1$, and matching coefficients gives $$Q_0=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma-q&-1/2\\-1/2&q\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q_1=(q),$$ with one free scalar $q$. At $q=\tfrac12$, $\sigma=1$ both matrices are PSD, no shift is needed, and $U=1=f^\star$.

Theorem 2 (exactness; no duality gap). Under the box assumption and $K\ne\emptyset$, $$\inf_\theta U(\theta)=\rho_r^{\rm tr},\qquad f^\star\le\rho_r^{\rm tr}\le\rho_r .$$ The primal problem $\rho_r^{\rm tr}$ attains its maximum. In the Boolean case $\rho_r^{\rm tr}=\rho_r$. If the trace cuts are inactive at some optimal solution of $\rho_r$, then $\rho_r^{\rm tr}=\rho_r$. If the quadratic module is Archimedean, $\rho_r^{\rm tr}\downarrow f^\star$ as $r\to\infty$ (for cliques, under running intersection [6]).

Proof idea. Write $\rho_r^{\rm tr}$ as a conic program, attach multipliers $\sigma$, $Q$'s and $t\ge0$ to $y_0=1$, the PSD blocks and the cuts, and eliminate $y$: the dual is $\min\sigma+\sum_k s_kt_k+\sum_is_iB_it_i$ subject to the coefficient identity and $Q+tI\succeq0$. For fixed $(\sigma,Q,p)$ the optimal $t$ is exactly the shift in $U$. The primal set is compact (the cuts bound the diagonals, $|y_{a+b}|\le\sqrt{y_{2a}y_{2b}}$ bounds the rest) and the dual strictly feasible, so conic duality [20] gives zero gap.

What the theorem says, and does not. $\inf_\theta U$ is not necessarily attained, and $\rho_r^{\rm tr}$ need not equal $\rho_r$ for non-Boolean problems; the cuts can only help. In all 23 problem–order pairs of the first experiment, they did not change the optimal value. The reformulation of trace-bounded SDPs as largest-eigenvalue minimization is classical [15][18]; what Theorems 1–2 add is that it applies to the whole sparse moment–SOS hierarchy, including localizing blocks and equalities, with box-derived weights instead of a constant-trace assumption. In the Boolean case both cuts are redundant: every monomial of $g_i$ is an off-diagonal entry of a moment block with unit diagonal, so $|y_\alpha|\le1$ and $\tr M_i^{\rm loc}(y)\le s_iB_i$ already follows from the relaxation.

Diagonal domination. The uniform shift $tI$ is one member of a family: shifting by $tD$ for a positive diagonal $D=\diag(\varrho)$ is also valid. Minimizing over the weights recovers the diagonal domination of Dathathri et al. [19] and, on the primal side, the textbook diagonal cuts $y_{2\alpha}\le1$:

Proposition (diagonal domination and diagonal cuts). Let $\delta^+(Q)=\min\{\mathbf 1^\top d:\ Q+\diag(d)\succeq0,\ d\ge0\}$ ($d$ free for $\delta^0$, used for Boolean cliques) and $U^{\rm d}(\theta)=\sigma+\sum_k\delta_k(Q_{0,k})+\sum_iB_i\,\delta^+(Q_i)$. Then (a) $f^\star\le U^{\rm d}(\theta)\le U_\varrho(\theta)\le U(\theta)$ for every weight vector, and $\inf_\varrho U_\varrho=U^{\rm d}$; (b) $\inf_\theta U^{\rm d}=\rho_r^{\rm d}$, the value of the moment relaxation with the diagonal cuts $M_k(y)_{aa}\le1$, $M_i^{\rm loc}(y)_{aa}\le B_i$, and $f^\star\le\rho_r^{\rm d}\le\rho_r^{\rm tr}\le\rho_r$; (c) in the Boolean case $\rho_r^{\rm d}=\rho_r^{\rm tr}=\rho_r$; (d) $U^{\rm d}$ is convex but not an eigenvalue function — each $\delta(Q)$ is a small SDP — so the augmented Lagrangian below applies with $t_bI$ replaced by $\diag(d_b)$.

The uniform shift is the cheapest first-order-friendly member of this family (one eigenpair per block), and it is lossless for Boolean problems by (c). The cuts are not a formality, however, when the box is not encoded in the constraints. Example: maximize $x_1+x_2$ subject to $x_1x_2=0$ on $[-1,1]^2$ ($f^\star=1$), without adding $1-x_i^2\ge0$. The quadratic module is not Archimedean and the Lasserre relaxation is unbounded at every order (the Dirac mass at $(t,0)$ is feasible for every $t$), so $\rho_r=+\infty$. With the trace cuts it is bounded, $\rho_1^{\rm tr}=\sqrt2$, $\rho_2^{\rm tr}=1.338$, $\rho_3^{\rm tr}=1.289$, and the diagonal cuts are strictly tighter from order 2: $\rho_2^{\rm d}=2^{1/4}=1.189$, $\rho_3^{\rm d}=2^{1/6}=1.122$. Similarly, for a box QP $\max x^\top Qx$ without the $n$ box constraints, $\rho_r=+\infty$ while $\rho_1^{\rm d}$ equals the Shor relaxation with box constraints: the diagonal cuts replace the $n$ localizing blocks by a single block. We use the uniform shift throughout, except where stated.

Formal Lagrangian and the Oracle

Free parametrization of the Gram freedom. Every polynomial $q$ supported on the products of basis monomials has a family of Gram matrices: the set of $(Q_{0,k})_k$ with $\sum_kv_k^\top Q_{0,k}v_k=q$ is an affine space $\{Q^{\rm sp}(q)+\Theta\}$, where $Q^{\rm sp}$ spreads each coefficient $q_\gamma$ evenly over the index pairs whose product is $x^\gamma$, and $\Theta$ ranges over zero-sum perturbations of each such group. Consequently every dual point is parametrized by $\theta=(\sigma,\Theta,u,p)$ with $u$ the entries of the localizing Gram matrices and $p$ the equality multipliers, and $\theta\mapsto(Q_{0,k},Q_i)$ is affine. Overlapping cliques share index-pair groups, which is how moment consistency across cliques (the sparse relaxation) enters.

Formal Lagrangian. Introduce pseudo-vectors $y^k\in\R^{s_k}$, $z^i\in\R^{s_i}$ and replace every monomial $x^{a+b}$ in the constraint identities by the product $y_ay_b$ (resp. $z_az_b$). The result is $$F(y,z;\theta)=\sigma-\sum_k y^{k\top}Q_{0,k}(\theta)y^k-\sum_iz^{i\top}Q_i(\theta)z^i,$$ so that $-\tfrac12\nabla^2_{y^k}F=Q_{0,k}(\theta)$: the Gram matrices are the Hessian blocks of the formal Lagrangian, and the hand-written Hankel, constant and localizing relations of the earlier version are not needed. Different factorization conventions for a moment $x^\gamma=x^ax^b$ only shift the base point and give the same set of dual points and the same $\inf U$. We checked the identity numerically: at random $\theta$ the residual at random points is $\le2\times10^{-15}$ for dense, sparse, Boolean, inequality- and equality-constrained instances.

Oracle cost. $U(\theta)=\sigma+\sum_bw_b\phi_b(\mathcal Q_b(\theta))$ is convex and piecewise smooth, with $w_b=s_k$ or $s_iB_i$. A subgradient is $e_\sigma-\sum_bw_b\mathcal Q_b'^{*}(v_bv_b^\top)$ over blocks with an active shift, $v_b$ being the smallest-eigenvalue eigenvector. One evaluation needs the smallest eigenpair of each block and a gather/scatter back-substitution. For blocks up to $s\approx2500$ a dense symmetric eigensolver is faster than Lanczos ($0.4$ s versus $1.7$ s at $s=1831$); memory per iteration is $O(s^2)$ for the block and $O(s^2)$ for the multipliers — against an interior-point Schur complement of dimension $m\sim s^2$ and storage $O(m^2)=O(s^4)$.

Smoothing, Complexity and Certification

Entropic smoothing. Because $\lmax$ is the maximum of a family of linear functions, Nesterov's smoothing [21][22] applies. For $\eta>0$ let $$\phi_\eta^{+}(Q)=\eta\log\big(1+\tr e^{-Q/\eta}\big),\qquad \phi_\eta^{0}(Q)=\eta\log\tr e^{-Q/\eta}$$ (smoothings of $\pos{-\lmin(Q)}$ and $-\lmin(Q)$) and $U_\eta=\sigma+\sum_bw_b\phi_{\eta,b}(\mathcal Q_b)$. Then:

  • Sandwich: $U\le U_\eta\le U+\eta\sum_bw_b\log(s_b+\epsilon_b)$ — so $U_\eta$ is itself a valid upper bound.
  • Gradient: $\nabla U_\eta=e_\sigma-\sum_bw_b\mathcal Q_b'^{*}(P_b)$ with the (sub-)density matrix $P_b=e^{-Q_b/\eta}/(\epsilon_b+\tr e^{-Q_b/\eta})$.
  • Smoothness: $\nabla U_\eta$ is Lipschitz with constant $\ell/\eta$, from the strong convexity of the negative von Neumann entropy.

Theorem 3 ($O(1/\varepsilon)$ iteration bound). Let $W=\sum_bw_b$, $S=\max_b\log(s_b+1)$, $\eta=\varepsilon/(2WS)$. Accelerated gradient descent with backtracking [23] on $U_\eta$ from $\theta_0$ satisfies $U(\theta_k)\le U(\bar\theta)+\varepsilon$ for every comparison point $\bar\theta$ once $k+1\ge\frac{4R}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\ell WS}$, with $R=\|\theta_0-\bar\theta\|$. Subgradient methods need $\Theta(1/\varepsilon^2)$ iterations in the worst case.

The bound is worst-case and needs the tiny $\eta=\varepsilon/(2WS)$. In practice the default solver runs L-BFGS on $U_\eta$ for a decreasing sequence $\eta\in\{10^{-1},3\cdot10^{-2},\dots,10^{-5}\}$ with warm starts (up to 500 iterations per stage) — no complexity guarantee, but faster. At every function evaluation the hard value $U(\theta)$ is computed from the same eigen-decomposition at no extra cost, so the best certified bound seen so far is always available.

Certification. If $f(K)\subseteq\mathbb Z$ (e.g. unweighted MAX-CUT), then $f^\star\le\lfloor U(\theta)\rfloor$ for every dual point; if a feasible $\hat x\in K$ has $f(\hat x)=\lfloor U(\theta)\rfloor$, then $\hat x$ is a global maximizer. This is how we turn a numerical bound into a proof of optimality. In floating point the certificate is exact up to the error of the dense symmetric eigensolver, many orders of magnitude below the margins in the experiments; we did not use interval arithmetic or a verified Cholesky factorization, which a fully rigorous certificate would add.

A rigorous a posteriori certificate

Theorem 1 is a statement in exact arithmetic, whereas the solver returns floating-point numbers: the Gram matrices $\hat Q_b$ assembled from $\hat\theta$ satisfy the coefficient identity only up to rounding, and the eigenvalues are computed approximately. Both effects can be bounded rigorously a posteriori, in the spirit of verified SDP bounds [24] and the rounding–projection scheme for exact SOS certificates [25], at a cost below one oracle call. We assume IEEE double precision and write $\gamma_m=mu/(1-mu)$ with $u=2^{-53}$.

Lemma (verified lower bound on $\lmin$). Let $A$ be symmetric with floating-point entries, $\alpha$ a float, and suppose the floating-point Cholesky factorization of $\mathrm{fl}(A+\alpha I)$ runs to completion with computed factor $\hat R$. Then for any $\nu\ge\|\hat R\|_F^2$, $$\lmin(A)\ \ge\ -\alpha-\gamma_{s+1}\,\nu-u\max_a|a_{aa}+\alpha| .$$

Why. By the backward-error theorem for Cholesky [26], $\hat R^\top\hat R=\tilde A+\Delta A$ with $|\Delta A|\le\gamma_{s+1}|\hat R|^\top|\hat R|$, hence $\|\Delta A\|_2\le\gamma_{s+1}\|\hat R\|_F^2$; the diagonal rounding of $A+\alpha I$ adds the last term.

Theorem 4 (rigorous certificate). Let $\hat\sigma$, symmetric floating-point $\hat Q_{0,k},\hat Q_i$ and polynomials $\hat p_j$ be arbitrary (for instance the solver output). Let $e$ be the residual of the Gram identity, computed in exact rational arithmetic, $E=\sum_\gamma|e_\gamma|$, and let $\ell_b\le\lmin(\hat Q_b)$ be verified lower bounds from the Lemma. Then $$f^\star\ \le\ U_{\rm rig}:=\hat\sigma+\sum_ks_k\,\Phi_k(-\ell_k)+\sum_is_iB_i\pos{-\ell_i}+E .$$

Why. For $x\in K$ the identity holds exactly up to $|e(x)|\le E$ on the box, and the argument of Theorem 1 applies verbatim with the verified lower bounds. Every double is a dyadic rational, so $e$ is computed exactly with integer arithmetic after scaling by a power of two; the verification costs one Cholesky factorization per block ($s^3/3$ flops), less than an eigen-decomposition. The theorem certifies the bound for the floating-point problem data as given (for MAX-CUT these are small integers and exact), and in all experiments $U_{\rm rig}-U$ lies between $10^{-13}$ and $10^{-8}$, so $\lfloor U_{\rm rig}\rfloor=\lfloor U\rfloor$ and the optimality certificates are rigorous.

Primal recovery and a two-sided certificate

The smoothed objective carries an approximate primal solution: the density matrices $P_b$ are, up to the weights, candidate moment and localizing matrices, and the gradient of $U_\eta$ measures exactly their infeasibility. Put $X_b=w_bP_b(\theta)$ and let $\bar y$ be the slot average of the clique blocks: each moment $y_\gamma$ is the weighted average of the entries $X_k[a,b]$ whose index pair multiplies to $x^\gamma$.

Proposition (primal recovery). Fix $\eta>0$ and $\theta$, and let $g=\nabla U_\eta(\theta)$.

(a) $U_\eta(\theta)=\max_P\{\sigma+\sum_bw_b(-\langle Q_b(\theta),P_b\rangle+\eta H_b(P_b))\}$ (Gibbs variational principle), and $\mathcal L_\eta(\theta,P)=\langle\theta,g(P)\rangle+L_{\bar y}(f)+\eta\sum_bw_bH_b(P_b)$.
(b) Stationarity is feasibility: $g_\sigma=1-\bar y_0$; $g_\Theta=0$ iff every $X_k$ has the moment structure $M_k(\bar y)$; $g_u=0$ iff $X_i=M_i^{\rm loc}(\bar y)$; $g_p=(L_{\bar y}(h_jx^\mu))$. Hence $g=0$ iff $\bar y$ is feasible for $\rho_r^{\rm tr}$, and then $U-\eta\sum_bw_b\log(s_b+\epsilon_b)\le L_{\bar y}(f)\le\rho_r^{\rm tr}\le U$.
(c) For every $\bar\theta$ with $\|\bar\theta-\theta\|\le R$ and $U(\bar\theta)\le\rho_r^{\rm tr}+\epsilon$: $\rho_r^{\rm tr}\ge U(\theta)-\eta\sum_bw_b\log(s_b+\epsilon_b)-R\|g\|-\epsilon$; moreover $(\sum_k\|M_k(\bar y)-X_k\|_F^2)^{1/2}\le\|g_\Theta\|$.
(d) Two-sided certificate. If the problem is Boolean with a single clique and no constraints, then $\bar y_0=1$ automatically, and with $\delta=\pos{-\lmin M(\bar y)}\le\|g_\Theta\|$ and $\tau=\delta/(1+\delta)$, $$(1-\tau)\,L_{\bar y}(f)+\tau f_\varnothing\ \le\ \rho_r\ \le\ U(\theta)\qquad\text{for every }\theta\text{ and }\eta,$$ where $f_\varnothing$ is the constant coefficient of $f$.

Idea of (d). The uniform measure on $\{\pm1\}^n$ has moment vector $e_\varnothing$ and moment matrix $I$. Mixing $\bar y$ with $e_\varnothing$, $\tilde y=(1-\tau)\bar y+\tau e_\varnothing$, gives $M(\tilde y)\succeq(\tau-(1-\tau)\delta)I=0$, so $\tilde y$ is feasible for $\rho_r$ and its objective value is a lower bound on the relaxation value.

Part (b) turns the gradient norm into a meaningful stopping criterion (it is the infeasibility of the recovered moment vector), and part (d) closes, for MAX-CUT-type problems, the gap left by the dual bound alone: a single dual iterate brackets the relaxation value from both sides, with no reference solver. For constrained or non-Boolean problems the same construction gives $\bar y$ and its infeasibility, but the repair step needs a strictly feasible moment vector with a known eigenvalue margin, which we do not pursue.

A smoothing-free alternative: the dual augmented Lagrangian. Smoothing has a structural weakness for problems with many constraint blocks: the bias bound is $\eta\sum_bw_b\log(s_b+1)$, the weights $w_b=s_bB_b$ are fixed by validity, and in the neural-network problems below $W$ is of order $10^3$ so $\eta\lesssim10^{-7}$ would be needed — extremely ill-conditioned. We therefore also solve the exact dual $$\min_{\theta,t}\ \sigma+\sum_bw_bt_b\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Q_b(\theta)+t_bI\succeq0,\ \ t_b\ge0$$ with an augmented Lagrangian on the PSD constraints (boundary-point / SDPNAL line [27][28][29]). Each inner problem is smooth and bound-constrained (L-BFGS-B), one inner iteration costs the same eigen-decomposition per block, the multipliers converge to optimal moment matrices, and the certified bound $U(\theta)$ is evaluated at every outer iterate, so the anytime property is retained. The penalty parameter matters a great deal: on one verification problem, $\rho_0=1,10$ gave no useful bound in 150 s, while $\rho_0=300,1000$ reached errors of $1.5\times10^{-6}$ and $4.8\times10^{-7}$. We chose $\rho_0=1000$ from this one instance; it is not guaranteed to transfer. On order-2 MAX-CUT it is again the best of $\{100,300,1000\}$, and the solver reaches certified gaps of $10^{-8}$, far beyond the smoothing solver (see E10). The diagonal-shift variant is obtained by replacing $t_bI$ with $\diag(d_b)$.

Experiments

Setup. Everything is implemented in Python (NumPy, SciPy) in the package msos, in a single process with 8 BLAS threads on a 14-core macOS machine with 36 GiB of RAM. The baseline is MOSEK 11.2 through CVXPY on a separately written moment-matrix model that shares no code with the dual construction; reported MOSEK times and memory include model construction. All MAX-CUT instances are unweighted random 3-regular graphs (networkx, seed 1, one instance per size). Timings were taken on a lightly loaded but not isolated machine, so they carry some noise. Interior-point reference: MOSEK is the reference in E1–E6 and the neural-network section; experiments E7–E10 were run on another machine without MOSEK and use Clarabel through CVXPY instead (same models, same dual values to solver accuracy where both were run), so their timings are not comparable with E1–E6.

Three facts emerge. (i) Whenever the box is encoded in the constraints, or the problem is Boolean, the three relaxations coincide to solver accuracy ($\le2\times10^{-8}$) on every instance: the cuts are inactive. (ii) Without the box constraints the plain relaxation is unbounded in every case (by construction; the interior-point status is not always reliable, however: a rerun of one unit test at $r=3$ returned a spurious finite value $0.009$, below the objective of a feasible point, instead of reporting unboundedness), the trace cuts give a finite but loose value ($8.10$ against $6.34$ for the box QP with $n=4$, $r=1$), and the diagonal cuts reproduce exactly the value of the formulation with the $n$ box constraints, also for the correlatively sparse chain problem. (iii) The first-order solvers reach their respective interior-point values: the smoothing solver within $1.2\times10^{-4}$ of $\rho^{\rm tr}$, the diagonal-shift ALM within $6.8\times10^{-7}$ of $\rho^{\rm d}$.

For box QPs at $r=2$, dropping the $n$ localizing blocks in favour of the diagonal shift leaves one block instead of $n+1$ and $23$–$33\%$ fewer multipliers, with the same bound to $\le1.7\times10^{-6}$. In time, the smoothing solver on the standard formulation remains the fastest first-order method, the two ALM variants reach $10^{-6}$–$10^{-7}$ with no uniform winner, and the interior-point solver is faster than both ALM variants at every $n$. The diagonal shift is therefore a modelling convenience and a tightening device when the box is not encoded, not a speed-up.

Application: Second-Order Relaxations for ReLU Networks

Semidefinite certification of neural networks is usually limited to a first-order (Shor-type) relaxation: the SDP of Raghunathan et al. [32] for robustness verification, whose first-order solution at scale is the topic of [19], and LipSDP [33] for Lipschitz constants. Polynomial formulations with higher-order relaxations exist [34][35] but are limited by the cost of the SDP solver. Can first-order solvers reach order 2 on such problems?

Polynomial encodings

Robustness verification. For a ReLU network $a_l=\max(W_la_{l-1}+b_l,0)$ and an $\ell_\infty$ ball around $x_0$, maximize $c^\top(W_La_{L-1}+b_L)$ with $c=e_{\rm wrong}-e_{\rm true}$; a negative maximum proves robustness. Interval bound propagation (IBP) gives a box; stable neurons are substituted away, and every unstable neuron becomes a variable $z$ with $z\ge0$, $z\ge\mathrm{pre}$, $z\,(z-\mathrm{pre})=0$ and the triangle inequality. The objective is linear and the constraints quadratic, so order 1 is the Shor-type SDP of the literature and order 2 is strictly stronger.

Lipschitz constants. For $f(x)=W_1\max(W_0x+b_0,0)$, every Jacobian is $W_1\operatorname{diag}(s)W_0$ with $s\in\{0,1\}^w$, hence $\mathrm{Lip}_2(f)^2\le L_{\rm pat}^2:=\max_{\|u\|\le1,\ s\in\{0,1\}^w}\|W_1\operatorname{diag}(s)W_0u\|^2$, with equality when all activation patterns are realized. With spins $t=2s-1$ this is a degree-4 POP in $(u,t)$ with a ball constraint and Boolean $t_j$ (mixed Boolean/continuous mode), so order 2 is the lowest order that makes sense. We compare with LipSDP-Neuron [33] and with $L_{\rm pat}^2$ computed by enumerating all $2^w$ patterns.

Interpretation. Software: polyopt

The method is released as the PyTorch package polyopt. A problem is written with operator overloading, and the relaxation, the dual parametrization and the solve are automatic:

import polyopt as po
x, y = po.variables(2)
f = x**2*y + x*y**2 - x - y
problem = po.Problem(f, [x**2 + y**2 <= 1], sense="max", box=(-1, 1))
res = po.solve(problem, order=2)       # smoothing + L-BFGS (or po.solve_alm)
print(res.bound)                        # certified upper bound (lower for "min")
x_hat, value = res.round()              # feasible point = other side of the gap

Design. Problem rescales the box to $[-1,1]^n$, normalizes constraints and objective, supports a per-variable Boolean mask (mixed problems), and optionally adds box constraints. Relaxation builds all index arrays with NumPy (cliques by min-fill-in chordal extension of the correlative-sparsity graph). Oracle evaluates $U(\theta)$ with a few gather/scatter operations in torch and a custom autograd function for the spectral part whose backward pass is the analytic $-P_b$ — no eigenvector derivatives, so repeated eigenvalues are harmless. Both solvers warm-start order $r$ from order $r-1$ by embedding the Gram matrices; for Boolean blocks the embedded point keeps the bound exactly. solve_alm(shift="diag") computes the diagonal-cut relaxation. polyopt.nn contains the encodings above with their MILP, PGD and LipSDP references. The NumPy package msos additionally provides the rigorous certificate (verify.rigorous_bound), the primal recovery (SpectralDual.primal_moments, two_sided) and a NumPy port of the augmented Lagrangian. Ten unit tests check polynomial arithmetic, the Gram identity at random points, gradients against finite differences, known bound values (triangle $9/4\to2$; $K_5$: $6.25\to6$), equality constraints, sparse versus dense cliques, rounding, and warm-start validity.

Discussion and Limitations

What the method is good for. It produces, at any moment of its run, a rigorous upper bound on the POP maximum by a procedure whose memory is $O(s^2)$ instead of $O(s^4)$. When the optimum is integral and a good feasible point is available (Boolean problems), this closes the gap with a proof: the order-2 relaxation proves optimality of the best cut on every instance up to $n=60$ we tried (and on all 15 instances of E10), at sizes where the interior-point solver cannot run on the same machine; for $n\le40$ the proof is rigorous in exact arithmetic. With the augmented Lagrangian the same oracle also delivers the relaxation value itself to $10^{-8}$ with a certificate, and for Boolean problems the primal recovery brackets it from both sides without any reference solver.

What it is not.

  1. It is not faster than an interior-point solver on small problems: the crossover is at $n\approx26$ for dense order-2 relaxations, and for small or well-sparsified blocks MOSEK remains faster.
  2. The accuracy of the smoothing solver is moderate: roughly $10^{-3}$–$10^{-4}$ above the relaxation value, and it is the only solver we ran at $n\ge40$. The augmented Lagrangian reaches $10^{-8}$ on MAX-CUT up to $n=30$, but it is slower to a first useful bound, depends on $\rho_0$, and was not run at larger $n$. The primal–dual bracket exists only for Boolean problems without constraints and is only as tight as the stationarity reached; for constrained problems we recover a moment vector and its infeasibility but no lower bound on the relaxation value. The lower bounds on $f^\star$ in the scaling table still come from local search, not from the dual iterates.
  3. It is not matrix-free in the strong sense: the Gram freedom has $\Theta(s^2)$ parameters, so memory is quadratic in the block size.
  4. Evidence for solver performance is limited: one random instance per size for $n\ge26$ (five per size for $n\le24$, with a spread of about a factor 2 in time) and wall-clock times on a shared machine. In the comparison with other first-order solvers the non-certified baselines were faster at every size, with parameters tuned per instance; we did not run SketchyCGAL with a sketch.
  5. Engineering limits: monomials are encoded in 64-bit integers, so Boolean mode supports $n\le63$ and non-Boolean mode $n\le26$ at $r=2$; the eigen-oracle is dense up to $s\approx2500$.
  6. The complexity theorem concerns accelerated gradient, which was the slowest solver in practice; L-BFGS, the fastest, has no guarantee here.
  7. On the neural-network problems, order 2 was solved to full accuracy only up to about $14$ variables. At $n=38$–$40$ the ALM had not converged after 25 min (the interior-point solver on the sparse relaxation needed the same time, with $17\times$ more memory), and at $n=60$ it failed. The penalty parameter was chosen on one instance; the networks are small, trained on synthetic data, and the interval-arithmetic boxes are loose.
  8. Whenever the box is encoded in the constraints, the trace and diagonal cuts did not bind on any test problem, so the box-derived weights bring no tightening over the textbook relaxation there; their benefit is confined to problems whose constraints do not imply the box (where the plain relaxation is unbounded) and to the modelling saving for box QPs. The diagonal shift requires the augmented Lagrangian; we have no eigenvalue-oracle solver for it.
  9. The rigorous certificate assumes IEEE arithmetic without underflow or overflow, certifies the bound for the floating-point problem data as given, and was run only up to $s=821$.

Outlook

A spectral bundle method [15][18] should combine the robustness of our oracle with faster high-accuracy convergence; term sparsity can reduce $s$ further; the oracle — one dense eigen-decomposition per block — is a natural fit for GPUs; adaptively chosen weights may sharpen non-Boolean relaxations; and extending the two-sided certificate beyond unconstrained Boolean problems (a strictly feasible moment vector with a known eigenvalue margin is all that is needed) would complete the primal–dual picture for constrained problems.
