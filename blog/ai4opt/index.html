Walking Between Moment-SOS Orders

Exact structure and learned basis growth for MAX-CUT. Growing the moment basis one monomial at a time turns out to be a cutting-plane method in disguise: forests gain nothing, triangles are four linear inequalities, and an odd cycle is certified with $2m-3$ monomials instead of $\binom m2$. We use that structure to learn which cuts to add, which beats the published sublevel-hierarchy heuristics by $2.5$–$6.5\times$ at a matched budget.

Tong Chen, Shaojie Bai, Raghavendra Selvan
University of Copenhagen

Introduction

The moment–sum-of-squares (moment-SOS) hierarchy of Lasserre and Parrilo [1][2] turns polynomial optimisation into a sequence of semidefinite programs (SDPs) of increasing order $d$, whose values converge monotonically to the global minimum, often in finitely many steps [3]. Its practical weakness is the granularity of that sequence. The order-$d$ moment matrix has size $\binom{n+d}{d}=O(n^d)$, so for most problems of interest even the step from order $1$ to order $2$ is out of reach — and nothing lies in between. Structure-exploiting variants, correlative [4][5] and term sparsity [6], fill the gap with hand-designed rules.

This article studies the gap as a path. Which sub-bases $\mathcal B$ with $\mathcal B_1\subseteq\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal B_2$ should one pay for? Every such $\mathcal B$ is a valid relaxation, so growing the basis one monomial at a time — a walk — is an anytime algorithm whose bound improves monotonically. The question is the order of growth.

For MAX-CUT we can answer it exactly, and the answer turns the walk into something familiar: a cutting-plane method in basis space. We then use that structure to build a scalable engine and to train a small policy that picks cuts.

Contributions.

  1. Exact structure of the walk for MAX-CUT. The walk relaxation decouples by parity; a triangle of pair monomials is the four triangle inequalities; a basis whose pair graph is a forest gains nothing, and a triangle cactus gains exactly its triangle cuts; this explains why one-step greedy selection is blind and why triangles are the right macro-actions; and an odd cycle $C_m$ is certified exactly by a triangulation with $2m-3$ instead of $\binom m2$ monomials. This connects the hierarchy to the classical SDP + metric-polytope approach to MAX-CUT [7][8].
  2. A scalable learned walk. Batches of triangle cuts on top of the $n$-dimensional Goemans–Williamson cone, with an exact all-triples separation oracle, and a small policy trained by behaviour cloning and REINFORCE on tiny random graphs only.
  3. Evaluation. Each theorem is checked numerically; the policy is compared with random, structural, maximum-violation and diversity-aware selectors on random graphs up to $n=150$ and deployed zero-shot on Gset instances with $800$–$1000$ vertices, including a triangle-free torus. It is also compared against the six published heuristics of the sublevel hierarchy [9] at a matched budget, and against the size of the dense order-2 program.

We are explicit about what is and is not claimed: the theory is for Boolean quadratic problems; the learned policy's gain over the strongest hand-designed selector is modest; and at Gset scale the interior-point solve, not the selection rule, is the bottleneck.

Related Work

Hierarchies and sparsity. Lasserre [1] and Parrilo [2] introduced the hierarchy; Laurent [10][11] surveys it and compares it with Sherali–Adams and Lovász–Schrijver for $0/1$ problems. Sparse variants exploit correlative [4][5] or term sparsity [6] through chordal extensions [12]. Our walk is an instance-adaptive, anytime counterpart, and the exact-structure theorem below makes the link to chordal structure precise through the line graph of the basis graph.

MAX-CUT relaxations. The Goemans–Williamson (GW) SDP [13], its strengthening with triangle and odd-cycle inequalities [14][15], and the SDP+metric branch-and-bound solver BiqMac [7] are the classical strong bounds; Anjos and Wolkowicz [8] study a second lifting. We show these cuts are literally moment-matrix blocks.

Scalable SDP. Spectral bundle methods [16] (which also introduced the Gset instances) and low-rank factorisations [17] scale GW but do not directly give the certified bound a growing basis needs; we use an interior-point solver [18] and report its scaling limit honestly.

Learning to cut. Learning cut selection for MILP [19][20] and the theory of cut selection [21] are closest in spirit; see [22] for a survey and [23] for RL over graphs.

Sublevel hierarchy. Our own earlier work [9] inserts intermediate relaxations between orders $d$ and $d+1$ by adding moment blocks over selected variable subsets (level $l$, depth $q$), chosen by heuristics for which it states that no optimality guarantee is known and whose search is left as an open problem. We show below that these subsets are clique actions on the basis graph, and compare them with learned selection of triangles.

Dynamic proofs. The closest work is Fawzi et al. [24], who contrast static proofs (a fixed level of a Sherali–Adams-type hierarchy [25]) with dynamic proofs built by a learned sequence of inference rules, with the bound improvement of a linear program as dense reward, a DQN agent whose value network is invariant to variable relabelling, and generalisation from small to larger graphs (maximum stable set). The linear programs they obtain are orders of magnitude smaller than the static ones at comparable or better bounds, and they name semidefinite proof systems as future work. We take that step: our relaxations are semidefinite (moment matrices), our problem is MAX-CUT, and in addition to learning we give an exact account of what each added basis element is and evaluate at Gset scale. Our reward, relabelling invariance and out-of-distribution protocol follow the same philosophy, and we report the analogue of their size comparison.

The Walk: Partial-Basis Relaxations

Consider $\rho=\min_{\mathbf x\in\mathbf K}f(\mathbf x)$ with $\mathbf K=\{g_i\ge0\}$. Let $\rho_d$ be the order-$d$ moment bound and $\mathcal V_d$ the monomials of degree $\le d$. For a finite set of monomials $\mathcal B$ with $\mathcal V_d\subseteq\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal V_{d+1}$, define $\rho(\mathcal B)$ as the minimum of $\mathcal L_{\mathbf y}(f)$ over pseudo-moments $\mathbf y$ with $y_0=1$, $M_{\mathcal B}(\mathbf y)\succeq0$ and $M_{\mathcal B_i}(g_i\mathbf y)\succeq0$, where $M_{\mathcal B}$ is the moment matrix restricted to rows and columns in $\mathcal B$.

Proposition (Sandwich). $\rho_d\le\rho(\mathcal B)\le\rho(\mathcal B')\le\rho_{d+1}\le\rho$ for $\mathcal V_d\subseteq\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal B'\subseteq\mathcal V_{d+1}$.

Why. A principal submatrix of a PSD matrix is PSD, so the constraints for $\mathcal B$ are implied by those for $\mathcal B'$; every feasible point of the larger relaxation is feasible for the smaller.

So $\rho(\mathcal B)$ is an anytime lower bound that any single added monomial can improve, and its SOS dual is a certificate that uses only squares of polynomials supported on $\mathcal B$. Choosing the order of growth is a sequential decision problem: state $=\mathcal B$, action $=$ a monomial (or group of monomials) in $\mathcal V_{d+1}\setminus\mathcal B$, deterministic transitions, and reward $r(\mathcal B,a)=\rho(\mathcal B\cup\{a\})-\rho(\mathcal B)-\lambda$ with a compute penalty $\lambda$; the return telescopes to the total bound improvement.

Our first experiments used a policy over single monomials and found that the marginal reward is usually exactly zero. The next section explains why, and what the right action space is.

Structure of the Walk for MAX-CUT

We specialise to the problem where the walk can be analysed exactly, $$\rho^\star=\min_{x\in\{\pm1\}^n}\ \sum_{i\lt j}W_{ij}\,x_ix_j ,$$ with $W$ symmetric and zero on the diagonal. This is MAX-CUT: a cut has value $\tfrac12(\sum_{i\lt j}W_{ij}-\sum_{i\lt j}W_{ij}x_ix_j)$, so a lower bound $\rho$ on the minimum is an upper bound $\tfrac12(\sum_{i\lt j}W_{ij}-\rho)$ on the maximum cut.

Modulo the Boolean ideal $\langle x_i^2-1\rangle$ every monomial is a square-free $x_S$, with $x_Sx_T=x_{S\triangle T}$. Write $Y_{ij}=\mathcal L_{\mathbf y}(x_ix_j)$. For a set $P$ of pair monomials take the basis $\mathcal B(P)=\{1\}\cup\{x_i\}\cup\{x_ax_b:ab\in P\}$ and write $\rho(P)$ for its walk relaxation. Then $\rho(\emptyset)$ is the GW bound [13] and $\rho(\binom{[n]}{2})=\rho_2$ is the order-2 Lasserre bound, so $$\rho(\emptyset)\le\rho(P)\le\rho_2\le\rho^\star\quad\text{for every }P.$$ We call $H_P=([n],P)$ the pair graph.

Parity decoupling

Proposition (Parity decoupling). $\rho(P)$ is the optimal value of $\min\langle W,Y\rangle$ subject to $Y\succeq0$, $\operatorname{diag}Y=\mathbf 1$, and $$N(Y,z)=\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf y_P^\top\\ \mathbf y_P&C(Y,z)\end{bmatrix}\succeq0,$$ where $(\mathbf y_P)_{ab}=Y_{ab}$, $C_{ab,ab}=1$, $C_{ab,cd}=Y_{xy}$ if the pairs share one index and $\{x,y\}=\{a,b\}\triangle\{c,d\}$, and $C_{ab,cd}=z_{abcd}$ is a free variable for disjoint pairs.

Why. Conjugating a feasible moment matrix by $D=\operatorname{diag}((-1)^{|S|})$ flips the sign of the odd moments without changing the objective, which only involves $|S|=2$. Averaging the two gives a feasible point of equal value whose odd moments vanish, so its moment matrix is block diagonal in the parity of the row degree: the odd rows $\{x_i\}$ give $Y$, the even rows $\{1\}\cup\{x_ax_b\}$ give $N$.

The walk therefore never needs the $(1+n+|P|)$-dimensional cone of the naïve formulation — only an $n$-dimensional cone and a $(1+|P|)$-dimensional one.

A triangle is four linear inequalities

Lemma (Triangle block). Let $ij,ik,jk\in P$. The principal submatrix of $N$ on the rows $1,\ x_ix_j,\ x_ix_k,\ x_jx_k$ is $$\begin{bmatrix}1&a&b&c\\a&1&c&b\\b&c&1&a\\c&b&a&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad a=Y_{ij},\ b=Y_{ik},\ c=Y_{jk},$$ with eigenvalues $1+a+b+c,\ 1+a-b-c,\ 1-a+b-c,\ 1-a-b+c$. It is PSD iff $(a,b,c)$ satisfies the four triangle inequalities, the facets of the metric polytope [15].

Why. The matrix is the group matrix of $\mathbb Z_2^2$; the Hadamard matrix diagonalises it. Dually, the inequality $1+\sigma_1a+\sigma_2b+\sigma_3c\ge0$ is certified by the square $\tfrac14(1+\sigma_1x_ix_j+\sigma_2x_ix_k+\sigma_3x_jx_k)^2$: a triangle is exactly the three monomials an SOS certificate needs to contain this square.

Try it. Move $(a,b,c)=(Y_{ij},Y_{ik},Y_{jk})$ and watch the four eigenvalues. The GW optimum for a unit-weight triangle sits at $(-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)$, which violates a triangle inequality; the cuts push it to the exact value $-1$.

What a basis graph buys: forests, cacti and cycles

Let $\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T)$ be the value of the cut relaxation $\{Y\succeq0,\operatorname{diag}Y=\mathbf1\}$ plus the four triangle inequalities of every triple in a set $\mathcal T$, and $\mathcal T(P)$ the triangles of $H_P$. By the Lemma, $\rho(P)\ge\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T(P))$.

Theorem 1 (Exact structure).

(a) If $H_P$ is a forest then $\rho(P)=\rho(\emptyset)$.
(b) If every block (biconnected component) of $H_P$ is an edge or a triangle — a triangle cactus — then $\rho(P)=\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T(P))$.
(c) In general $\rho(P)\ge\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T(P))$, and the inequality can be strict.

Proof idea. By a Schur complement, $N\succeq0$ iff $\tilde P=C-\mathbf y_P\mathbf y_P^\top\succeq0$. Its specified entries live on the line graph of $H_P$; for a forest or a triangle cactus this line graph is chordal, with maximal cliques the vertex stars and the triangles. By the theorem of Grone et al. [26], a partial matrix with chordal pattern is PSD-completable iff every maximal-clique block is PSD. Star blocks are automatically PSD (a Schur complement of $Y$), and a triangle block is PSD iff the four triangle inequalities hold. For (c), $C_5$ plus one chord reaches the exact value $-3$, strictly better than the single triangle.

Corollary (Plateaus and non-myopia). If $P$ is a forest, every single pair $e$ with $P\cup\{e\}$ a forest has zero marginal gain. For a triangle $\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}$ the gains of $\{e_1\}$ and $\{e_1,e_2\}$ are $0$, while adding $e_3$ gives a strictly positive gain exactly when no optimum of the current relaxation satisfies the triangle inequalities. The marginal-gain function is not submodular, and a one-step greedy rule on pair monomials is blind (every candidate ties at zero) at every state whose pair graph is a forest.

This formalises the empirical failure of one-step greedy selection: the natural action is the triangle, a macro-action of three monomials, and when triangles are glued as a cactus the relaxation is exactly the corresponding cutting-plane relaxation.

Proposition (Progress certificate). $\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T\cup\{\tau\})>\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T)$ iff every optimal $Y$ of the current relaxation violates a triangle inequality of $\tau$. In particular, violation at the computed solution is necessary for progress, and sufficient when the optimum is unique.

This tells us where to look: only triples violated by the current solution can help.

Theorem 2 (Odd cycles are certified by a triangulation). Let $C=(v_1,\dots,v_m)$ be an odd signed cycle and let $P$ contain its edges and the chords of a triangulation of the polygon $C$ ($m$ sides, $m-3$ chords, $m-2$ triangles). Then every feasible $Y$ satisfies $\sum_{e\in C}\varepsilon_eY_e\ge-(m-2)$. Consequently, for the odd cycle graph $C_m$ with unit weights, $\rho(P)=-(m-2)=\rho^\star$ with $2m-3$ monomials, whereas the GW bound is $-m\cos(\pi/m)$ and the order-2 relaxation uses $\binom m2$ pair monomials.

Proof idea. Induct on $m$ by cutting off an ear $(u,v,w)$: the triangle inequality for the right sign pattern lets us replace the two ear edges by the chord, giving a signed polygon with $m-1$ sides and the same parity. The base case $m=3$ is a triangle inequality; the cut that leaves a single edge uncut attains the bound.

Learning the Walk

Macro-action MDP. Rewards are non-zero only when a whole triangle is present, so the action is a triple $\{i,j,k\}$, which adds the three monomials $x_ix_j,x_ix_k,x_jx_k$. When triangles are glued as a cactus the relaxation is exactly the cut relaxation $\rho_{\rm cut}$ — one $n$-dimensional cone plus four linear inequalities per triangle, instead of an extra cone of dimension $3|\mathcal T|$. In general $\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T)\le\rho(P(\mathcal T))$ is a valid but weaker relaxation; at scale we always use $\rho_{\rm cut}$. A round adds a batch of $K$ triples and re-solves once; a walk is $R$ rounds. Inactive cuts (zero dual in all four inequalities) are discarded after every solve, for all methods.

Candidates by exact separation. There are $\binom n3$ triples, but the progress certificate says only violated ones can help. We compute the violation $v(\tau)=\max_{\sigma}\{-1-\sigma\cdot(Y^\star_{ij},Y^\star_{ik},Y^\star_{jk})\}$ of all triples with blocked tensor operations ($O(n^3)$, 9 s for $n=800$ on one machine) and keep the $M$ most violated as the candidate pool. This also covers triples containing non-edges, so the method applies to triangle-free graphs.

Policy. A shared MLP $s_\theta(\tau)$ scores candidates from size-agnostic features: normalised violation and its rank in the pool; the number of edges of $G$ among the three pairs; the alignment of the violated cut with the objective; summed edge weight; mean $|Y^\star|$ on the three pairs; normalised degrees; the number of already-active cuts touching the pairs; and the round index. A batch is built sequentially by sampling from $\mathrm{softmax}(s_\theta)$ after each pick, with two within-round overlap features (how many picked triples share a pair, resp. a vertex, with $\tau$) updated after every pick, which lets the policy learn diversity. At test time the batch is built greedily.

Proposition (Relabelling equivariance). Let $\pi$ be a permutation of $[n]$ and relabel the instance by $W\mapsto\pi W\pi^\top$, $Y\mapsto\pi Y\pi^\top$. For every candidate $\tau$ the score $s_\theta(\tau)$ is unchanged when $\tau$ is relabelled by $\pi$; hence the batch-selection distribution is equivariant: relabelling the instance relabels the selected triples and does not change the bound sequence.

Why. Every feature is a symmetric function of the three pairs of $\tau$ (violation and its rank in the pool, the number of edges of $G$ among the pairs, the alignment sum, mean $|Y_e|$, degrees of the three vertices, counts of already-selected triples sharing a pair or vertex, and pool-level statistics), so it does not depend on vertex names; the network is applied row-wise, and the sequential sampler uses only these scores. This plays the role of the two symmetries (orderless sets, variable relabelling) built into the value network of [24]; the invariance holds by construction and we confirm it numerically (maximum score difference $6\times10^{-8}$, float32 round-off, over $2{,}927$ triples of a random $n=30$ instance after a random relabelling). Because the features do not depend on $n$, the same $\theta$ applies to any graph size, which is what enables the zero-shot protocol below.

Optional pooled context. Following the set-pooled memory encoding of [24], we also tested a variant in which every candidate additionally sees a pooled summary of the active cut set and of the candidate pool (active-cut count relative to $n$, mean normalised pool violation, fraction of pool triples that are graph triangles, and the pooled vertex load of the active cuts at the candidate's vertices). It gives no measurable gain ($+0.06\pm0.10$ points, see the ablation below), so all main results use the policy without it.

Training. We first behaviour-clone, on each training graph, the better of two hand-designed selectors (struct and nms, below), then fine-tune with REINFORCE [27]: four rollouts per graph, return $=(\rho_{\rm final}-\rho_{\rm ref})/(\rho_{\rm ref}-\rho_{\rm GW})$ with $\rho_{\rm ref}$ the better of the two selectors on that graph, and the mean over the four rollouts as baseline. Training uses only random graphs with $30\le n\le80$, edge probability log-uniform in $[0.04,0.5]$, unit or $\pm1$ weights, $K=n$ and $R=4$ rounds — behaviour cloning on $160$ graphs, then $45$ REINFORCE iterations of $14$ graphs, about $25$ minutes on 14 cores.

Baselines. random: $K$ uniformly random triples. struct: triples ranked by the number of edges of $G$ they contain (the structural triangle heuristic, extended to non-edges). top-K: the $K$ most violated triples, the standard cutting-plane rule. nms: most-violated triples subject to no two sharing a pair (“non-maximum suppression”), a common diversity heuristic. All methods use identical solves, cut dropping and budget $(K,R)$.

Experiments

All SDPs are solved with MOSEK 11 [18] (Fusion API, native symmetric cones; the same relaxations through a modelling layer that adds $O(n^2)$ symmetry rows were more than $300\times$ slower at $n=200$). Reported bounds are dual objective values, certified up to the solver tolerance $10^{-7}$.

Validating the theory

  • Parity decoupling. The decoupled engine and the naïve $(1+n+|P|)$-cone engine agree to $3\times10^{-7}$.
  • Forests and cacti. Over 25 random weighted instances ($n=10$) with random forests, $\max|\rho(P)-\rho(\emptyset)|=5\times10^{-6}$; for 25 random triangle cacti, $\max|\rho(P)-\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T(P))|=3\times10^{-6}$, while the cuts themselves improve the GW bound by $0.14$ on average — the equality is not vacuous.
  • General pair graphs. For 40 random sets of triples ($n=9$), $\rho(P)\ge\rho_{\rm cut}(\mathcal T(P))$ always, and the inequality is strict in $52\%$ of cases: cycles of length $\ge4$ in $H_P$ contribute. The sharpest example: $C_5$ plus one chord reaches the exact value $-3$ with $6$ monomials, whereas the single triangle's cuts give only $-3.83$.
  • Odd cycles. For every odd $m\le21$ the fan triangulation ($2m-3$ monomials) attains exactly $-(m-2)$ (error $<10^{-8}$); the full order-2 relaxation needs $\binom m2$ monomials ($210$ vs. $39$ at $m=21$). Exhaustive search over chord sets shows the triangulation count is not minimal: $C_5$, $C_7$, $C_9$ reach the exact value with $1$, $2$, $3$ chords, i.e. $\lceil(m-3)/2\rceil$ chords, and never with one fewer. We leave a proof to future work.
  • Ceilings. On $12$ small random graphs ($n\in\{10,12,14\}$), the exact order-2 bound equals the brute-force optimum to $2\times10^{-6}$ and the full triangle-metric bound to $1.3\times10^{-4}$, while GW is $0.004$–$1.7$ below. In this regime the ceiling is reached with triangle cuts alone, and the walk's task is purely how quickly the cuts are found.

The learned policy is the best method in every setting, ahead of the best hand-designed selector on each graph by $4.4$ to $7.6$ points, and winning on all $72$ graphs. The baselines are informative on their own. Random selection closes only $11$–$32\%$, confirming that which triples matter. Two ablations isolate what is responsible. Behaviour cloning alone essentially reproduces the better of its two teachers (within $0.7$ points of the best baseline mean in five of six settings), so the extra $4$–$6$ points come from the REINFORCE stage. And a first version trained only against the violation selector ($n\le60$, $p\ge0.1$) gained just $1.5$–$3.0$ points over nms and was below the structural rule on sparse graphs ($79.9$ vs. $81.7$ at $n=100$) — which is why the training distribution and the reference were changed.

Size of the relaxation: static versus dynamic

Fawzi et al. [24] measure a dynamic proof by the size of the program it needs against a static hierarchy level. The analogue here is the dense order-2 relaxation, whose parity-reduced cone has dimension $1+\binom n2$ (and $1+n+\binom n2$ before reduction), against the walk's $n$-dimensional cone plus $4$ linear rows per active cut. After six rounds the learned walk has a cone dimension $20\times$ (at $n=40$) to $75\times$ (at $n=150$) smaller than order 2, plus a linear block of $4|\mathcal T|\le3{,}600$ rows, while closing $87$–$95\%$ of the gap to the triangle ceiling on average. The comparison is of sizes, not of bounds: dense order 2 is at least as tight as the ceiling by construction, and at $n=800$ (Gset) its cone has dimension $319{,}601$, which is out of reach for the solver used here, whereas the walk's longest master problem is an $800$-dimensional cone ($400\times$ smaller) with $16{,}000$ rows.

Removing the violation features costs $13.0\pm0.8$ points (worse on all $72$ graphs), removing the graph-structure features $6.7\pm0.3$ points (worse on all $72$), so the policy uses both sources of information. Two additions did not help: the overlap features (difference $-0.1\pm0.1$, $30/72$ wins) and the pooled context ($+0.06\pm0.10$, $39/72$ wins) — within-round diversity is not something the policy needs explicit features for, and the pooled memory encoding of [24] does not transfer to this setting. Behaviour cloning alone is $3.7\pm0.2$ points below the full pipeline. With one training run per variant, differences below about one point should not be interpreted.

Comparison with the sublevel hierarchy

The sublevel hierarchy [9] also interpolates between orders $1$ and $2$: it selects $q$ variable subsets $\Gamma_1,\dots,\Gamma_q$ of size $l$ (level and depth) and adds to Shor's relaxation one moment block over $\{1\}\cup\{x_ax_b:a,b\in\Gamma_j\}$ for each subset. That paper states that no algorithm for choosing the subsets with guarantees is known, proposes heuristics H1–H6, and lists a search for the level, depth and subsets as an open problem; it also reports that the random heuristic H1 performs best on average. Our results give the following reading.

Proposition (Sublevel subsets are clique actions). In block form the order-1 sublevel relaxation is $Y\succeq0$, $\operatorname{diag}Y=\mathbf 1$ and $N_{K_l(\Gamma_j)}\succeq0$ for $j\le q$, where $N_{K_l(\Gamma)}$ is the block of the parity-decoupling proposition for the complete pair graph on $\Gamma$. (i) For $l=2$ it equals Shor's bound; for $l=3$ it equals the four triangle cuts of $\Gamma$. (ii) For $l\ge4$ every one of the $\binom l3$ triples of $\Gamma$ yields its four triangle cuts (principal $4\times4$ minors), so the gain is at least that of the cuts on triples inside $\Gamma$ and arises only from those violated at the current solution (progress certificate).

Why. (i): the block of a single pair is trivial; the block of a triangle is a triangle block, hence its four inequalities. (ii): principal submatrices of a PSD block are PSD.

Thus a subset spends $\binom l2$ pair monomials on at most $\binom l3$ cuts, selected through a vertex set whose triangles need not be violated, while our walk chooses the violated triples directly. The proposition also says the heuristics H1–H6 can only be as good as the violation mass of the triples their subsets happen to contain.

Protocol. We implement the block relaxation exactly and the heuristics H1 (random subsets), H2 (ordered windows), H3 (moment: $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ of the Shor moment submatrix), H4 (Laplacian) and H5/H6 (windows contained in many/few maximal cliques of a chordal extension of the correlative-sparsity graph), following [9]. As the BiqMac instances used there are not available offline, we generate the same families with the same densities: g05 ($p=0.5$, unit weights), pm1s ($p=0.1$, $\pm1$), pm1d ($p=0.99$, $\pm1$), plus weighted dense families w05 (integer weights in $[-10,10]$) and pw05 (in $[1,10]$); $n\in\{60,80,100\}$, $10$ graphs per (family, $n$) cell, i.e. $30$ per family and $150$ in total. Configurations: $(l,q)\in\{(4,2),(6,2),(8,1),(6,6)\}$. All selectors choose one-shot from the Shor solution. The budget is matched in pair monomials: the sublevel uses at most $q\binom l2$ ($12$, $30$, $28$, $90$) and our selectors pick $K=\lfloor q\binom l2/3\rfloor$ triples. We report the improvement of the min-sum bound over Shor (twice the MAX-CUT upper-bound improvement), for our two selectors (violation with pair-disjointness, “nms”, and the learned policy used unchanged, with no retraining) solved as triangle cuts. Solving the same triples as one exact walk block changes the result by $0.05$ on average ($\le1.5$ at most; equal when the selected triangles are edge-disjoint, as the cactus theorem predicts).

Results.

  1. Over all $600$ (graph, configuration) cells the learned policy beats the per-graph best of the six sublevel heuristics in every cell (ratio of means $2.5\times$–$6.5\times$; median per-cell ratio $3.6\times$, minimum $1.2\times$), and so does the pair-disjoint violation rule.
  2. The learned policy beats the violation rule in $425$ of $600$ cells ($+1.57$ on average), but the gain is concentrated: $119$ of $120$ cells on the sparse family pm1s, versus about half of the cells on the dense unweighted families g05 ($64/120$) and pm1d ($72/120$), where the two are effectively tied.
  3. Among the heuristics there is no consistent winner, as reported in [9]: relative to random subsets (H1) the others are worse on average ($-0.5\pm0.1$ for H4/H5, $-1.0\pm0.1$ for H2, $-1.4\pm0.1$ for H3, $-0.8\pm0.1$ for H6, paired over all cells) — using the Shor moments or the Laplacian to choose subsets does not help, and H3 is the worst.
  4. A mechanism consistent with the proposition: at $n=80$, $l=6$, $q=2$ the triples inside the subsets contain total violation $3.2$ (H1), $2.9$ (H2), $1.8$ (H3), $3.2$ (H4) over $20$–$30$ pair monomials, against $4.9$ over $30$ pairs for the $10$ pair-disjoint most-violated triples; about half of the triples inside a random subset are violated by more than $10^{-6}$, so the loss is in the amount of violation captured per pair monomial, not in finding any violated triple.

Caveats. The families are re-generated, not the BiqMac files, so absolute numbers are not comparable with [9]; the budget is matched in pair monomials, not in solve time; heuristics are one-shot as specified, whereas ours can additionally be iterated; and the best-of-six per graph oracle is generous to the baselines. In absolute terms all selectors remove a small part of the Shor gap at these budgets.

Zero-shot deployment on Gset

We deploy the policy trained on $n\le80$ graphs, unchanged, on four Gset instances: G1 (dense, unit weights, $19{,}176$ edges), G14 (union of planar-like graphs, $4{,}694$ edges), G43 ($n=1000$, $9{,}990$ edges) and G11 (a $\pm1$ toroidal grid with no triangles). Each round adds $K=500$ cuts from a pool of $M=12{,}000$ most-violated triples; exact separation over all $\approx8.5\times10^7$ triples takes $9$ s at $n=800$, and a full round takes $7$–$77$ s depending on the number of active cuts (single machine, 14 cores). Because G11 has no triangles, an edge-restricted walk could not run on it at all; the all-triples oracle treats it like any other instance.

  1. With identical budgets the learned policy gives the tightest bound on G14, G43 and the triangle-free torus G11, and is within one unit of the best (nms) on G1 ($12010.1$ vs. $12009.2$).   3. The methods differ most where structure matters: on G14 and G11 the structural rule is far ahead of the violation-based selectors ($28.7\%$ vs. $25.0\%$ and $66.2\%$ vs. $41.0$–$49.7\%$ of the gap), while on the dense G1 the order reverses.   4. Random selection is useless at scale: on G14 it closes $0.1\%$ of the gap after three rounds.
  5. In absolute terms the gaps remain large — $14$–$67\%$ closed with at most $4{,}000$ cuts — and reaching the metric-polytope bound needs far more cuts than an interior-point master problem can hold ($>9$ GB and $350$ s per solve at $5{,}000$ cuts on G14). Making the walk competitive at that scale needs a different master solver, not a different selection rule.

Discussion

What the walk is. For MAX-CUT the walk between orders $1$ and $2$ of the hierarchy is, to a precise extent, a cutting-plane method: pair monomials appear in groups of three that are four linear inequalities each; forests carry no information; triangle cacti are exactly equal to their cuts; and cycles of length $\ge4$ in the basis graph give extra strength that cuts alone do not capture. This reframes “learning which monomials to add” as “learning which cuts to add”, links the SOS hierarchy to the SDP+metric methods of Rendl et al. [7], and tells us what a one-step greedy rule cannot see.

What is learned. Ablations show that the violation and graph-structure features both carry signal ($-13.0$ and $-6.7$ points when removed), while overlap features and pooled context add nothing measurable. A small MLP over size-agnostic features, trained for about $20$ minutes on graphs with at most $80$ vertices, selects cuts better than random, structural, violation and diversity-aware selectors on random graphs up to $150$ vertices and on three of four Gset instances (a near-tie on the fourth). The gains over the best hand-designed baseline are $4$–$8$ points of gap closed after six rounds on random graphs and a small but consistent edge at Gset scale ($-0.2$ to $+1.4$ points after $8$ rounds, the negative value being the near-tie on G1).

Relation to the sublevel hierarchy. The open problem of [9] — how to choose level, depth and subsets — is answered here in the following restricted sense: subsets are clique actions, and selecting violated triples directly with a pair budget gains $2.5\times$–$6.5\times$ more than the best of the six published heuristics on $150$ random instances; the heuristics do not beat random subsets on average. We have not tried to learn the level and depth themselves, nor mixed actions (triangles and larger cliques).

Limitations.

  • The theory is for Boolean quadratic problems. For general polynomial problems the parity and triangle structure is replaced by localizing and moment-matrix structure, and the right macro-action is not known.
  • Theorem 1(b) is exact only for triangle cacti; the scalable engine solves the cut relaxation, which is weaker than the exact walk when triangles share edges. We quantified this on small instances but did not scale the exact walk.
  • The conjectured minimal chord count $\lceil(m-3)/2\rceil$ for odd cycles is unproved.
  • At Gset scale the bottleneck is the interior-point master problem, so absolute bound improvements are small and we do not claim state-of-the-art bounds. First-order or bundle solvers with cut management [16] are the natural next step, and the learned selector is solver-agnostic.
  • Training cost (about $20$ minutes on $14$ cores) and the choice of reference selectors for the REINFORCE baseline affect the outcome: a first version trained against violation-only selectors fell below the structural rule on sparse graphs.
  • Only one problem class and a single batch size per round were studied.

Outlook

Combining the learned selector with a scalable cut-aware solver, extending the characterisation to the next layer of the hierarchy (pentagonal and hypermetric inequalities correspond to larger basis cliques), and proving the cycle conjecture are the most promising continuations.
